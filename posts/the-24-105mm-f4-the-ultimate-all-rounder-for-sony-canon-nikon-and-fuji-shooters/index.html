The 24-105mm F4: The Ultimate All-Rounder for Sony, Canon, Nikon, and Fuji Shooters

2026-08-10 · Lenses · Tags: The, lenses

Every photographer eventually faces the same dilemma: which lens can do it all without compromising on quality? The 24-105mm f/4 zoom has long been the answer for full-frame shooters, offering a versatile range that spans wide-angle to short telephoto. But with the rise of mirrorless systems from Sony, Canon, Nikon, and Fuji, this classic focal length has been reimagined for a new generation. In this article, we’ll explore why the 24-105mm f/4 (or its APS-C equivalent) has become the go-to choice for travel, events, and everyday photography. We’ll compare the best options across the four major systems, discuss how the constant f/4 aperture interacts with lighting setups, and show you how to get the most out of these lenses in your editing workflow. Whether you’re a working professional or an enthusiastic hobbyist, this guide will help you decide if the 24-105mm f/4 deserves a permanent spot in your camera bag.

The Appeal of the 24-105mm F4: Versatility Without Compromise

The 24-105mm focal range is a sweet spot for photographers who need to adapt quickly to changing scenes. At 24mm, you can capture sweeping landscapes, architectural details, and group shots. At 105mm, you can isolate subjects with a pleasing background blur, making it ideal for portraits and street photography. The constant f/4 aperture means your exposure settings remain consistent throughout the zoom range, which is a lifesaver when you’re shooting in variable light or using manual mode. Unlike variable-aperture kit lenses, the f/4 allows you to maintain a fast enough shutter speed in low light without constantly adjusting your ISO or aperture. This makes the 24-105mm f/4 a true all-rounder, perfect for travel where you might not have time to swap lenses, or for event photography where the action is unpredictable.

Cross-System Comparison: Sony, Canon, Nikon, and Fuji

Each major mirrorless system offers its own take on the 24-105mm f/4 concept. For Sony full-frame users, the FE 24-105mm f/4 G OSS is a standout. It features built-in optical stabilization, which is crucial for handheld video and low-light shooting. The lens is sharp from corner to corner, with minimal chromatic aberration, and its autofocus is fast and silent—ideal for hybrid shooters. Canon’s RF 24-105mm f/4 L IS USM is part of the prestigious L-series, offering exceptional build quality and weather sealing. It also includes a control ring that can be customized for aperture, ISO, or exposure compensation, a feature that Canon shooters love. The optical performance is superb, with creamy bokeh and excellent contrast. Nikon’s Z 24-120mm f/4 S is technically a 24-120mm, but it’s the closest equivalent in the Z lineup. It offers a slightly longer reach, which is great for portraits, and it’s one of the sharpest lenses in the S-line. The Z mount’s large diameter allows for excellent light transmission, and the lens is remarkably compact for its range. For Fuji shooters, the XF 16-80mm f/4 R OIS WR is the APS-C equivalent, providing a 24-120mm full-frame equivalent field of view. It’s weather-resistant, has optical stabilization, and is designed to pair perfectly with Fuji’s X-Trans sensors. While it’s not as fast as some primes, it’s a fantastic travel companion.

When comparing these lenses, consider the following factors:

  • Image Stabilization: Sony and Fuji include OIS, while Canon and Nikon rely on in-body stabilization (IBIS) in their cameras. If you’re using a body without IBIS, the Sony or Fuji lenses have an advantage.
  • Build Quality: Canon’s L-series and Nikon’s S-line are built to professional standards, with weather sealing and metal mounts. Sony’s G lens is also robust, but Fuji’s is slightly more consumer-oriented.
  • Autofocus Speed: All four lenses are quick, but Canon and Sony have the edge in low-light tracking, thanks to their advanced motor designs.
  • Price: Fuji’s lens is the most affordable, while Canon and Nikon are premium. Sony sits in the middle.

Ultimately, the best choice depends on your camera system and your specific needs. If you’re already invested in a system, the native lens is the obvious pick. But if you’re switching systems, these lenses are a strong reason to consider each brand.

Lighting Considerations: Working with the Constant F4 Aperture

The f/4 aperture is a double-edged sword. On one hand, it’s fast enough for most daylight and indoor situations, especially with modern sensors that handle high ISO well. On the other hand, it’s not as fast as an f/2.8 zoom or an f/1.8 prime, so you’ll need to be more deliberate with your lighting. In a studio setting, the constant f/4 is actually an advantage because it allows you to use a single exposure setting across a range of focal lengths, simplifying your workflow. With strobes or continuous lights, you can set your flash power and aperture once and then zoom without recalculating. For natural light photography, you’ll need to be mindful of shutter speed and ISO. In low light, you might have to push your ISO higher than you would with a faster lens, but modern cameras like the Sony A7 IV, Canon R6 II, Nikon Z6 III, and Fuji X-T5 handle ISO 3200 or 6400 remarkably well. If you’re shooting in very dim conditions, consider pairing the 24-105mm f/4 with a fast prime like a 35mm f/1.8 for those critical moments. Alternatively, you can use a speedlight or a small LED panel to supplement the ambient light. The key is to understand that the f/4 lens is not a limitation—it’s a tool that encourages you to think about light more creatively.

Editing Workflow: How Lens Character Shapes Your Post-Processing

Every lens has a unique “character” that affects your images—sharpness, contrast, color rendition, and bokeh. The 24-105mm f/4 lenses from Sony, Canon, Nikon, and Fuji are all modern designs that are clinically sharp, but they differ in subtle ways. For example, Canon’s L-series tends to produce warmer tones, while Sony’s G lens is more neutral. Nikon’s S-line is known for its high micro-contrast, which gives images a three-dimensional pop. Fuji’s lens, when paired with X-Trans sensors, produces beautiful film-like colors straight out of camera. When editing, you can use these characteristics to your advantage. In Lightroom or Capture One, you can create a preset that enhances the lens’s natural strengths. For instance, if you’re using the Canon lens, you might add a slight warm tint to your shadows to emphasize the creamy bokeh. With the Sony lens, you might increase clarity to bring out the fine details. The key is to not overdo it—these lenses are already sharp, so you don’t need to add excessive sharpening. Instead, focus on color grading and tonal adjustments. Also, be aware of lens corrections. Most modern software automatically applies profiles for these lenses, correcting distortion and vignetting. However, some photographers prefer to leave a little vignette for a more artistic look. Experiment with the amount of correction to find what suits your style.

Real-World Scenarios: Putting the 24-105mm F4 to Work

Let’s look at three common scenarios where the 24-105mm f/4 shines. Travel Photography: When you’re exploring a new city, you don’t want to carry a bag full of primes. The 24-105mm covers everything from narrow streets (24mm) to distant landmarks (105mm). The constant f/4 allows you to shoot handheld in museums and churches without a tripod, thanks to image stabilization and high ISO. Event Photography: At a wedding or corporate event, you need to be ready for wide group shots and tight candid portraits. The 24-105mm lets you switch instantly without missing a moment. The f/4 aperture is sufficient for most indoor lighting, especially if you’re using a flash or the venue has decent ambient light. Portrait Photography: While a 85mm f/1.4 is the classic portrait lens, the 105mm end of the 24-105mm f/4 can produce lovely headshots with a soft background. The compression at 105mm is flattering, and the f/4 aperture, when used close to your subject, can still create a shallow depth of field. For environmental portraits, the 24-35mm range is perfect for showing the subject in their surroundings. In all these scenarios, the lens’s versatility means you can focus on composition and storytelling rather than worrying about gear changes.

Conclusion

The 24-105mm f/4 (or its APS-C equivalent) is more than just a convenience—it’s a creative tool that encourages you to see the world in a flexible, dynamic way. Whether you choose Sony’s G lens, Canon’s L-series, Nikon’s S-line, or Fuji’s XF version, you’re getting a high-quality optic that can handle 90% of your photography needs. The constant f/4 aperture, combined with modern sensor technology and editing software, makes this lens a viable alternative to a bag full of primes. Of course, there are times when you’ll want a faster lens for extreme low light or a prime for ultimate sharpness, but for most photographers, the 24-105mm f/4 is the ultimate all-rounder. So, if you’re looking to simplify your kit without sacrificing image quality, this is the lens to consider. Happy shooting!
