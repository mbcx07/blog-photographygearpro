The Five Classic Portrait Lighting Patterns

Understanding lighting patterns is the foundation of portrait photography. Each pattern creates a different mood and flatters different face shapes. Memory Card en Amazon. Here's how to master them all.

1. Rembrandt Lighting

The look: A triangle of light on the shadow side of the face, under the eye. Named after the Dutch painter who used this pattern extensively.

Setup:
- Key light at 45 degrees to the subject, slightly above eye level
- The light should be positioned so the nose shadow connects with the cheek shadow
- A reflector or fill light on the opposite side at half power

Best for: Dramatic portraits, masculine subjects, strong character studies
Flatters: Oval and round faces
Avoid for: Very thin faces (can look gaunt)

Pro tip: The triangle should be no wider than the eye and no longer than the nose. If it's too large, move the light closer to camera. If too small, move it further away.

2. Loop Lighting

The look: A small shadow from the nose that loops down onto the cheek without connecting to the cheek shadow. The most commonly used portrait pattern.

Setup:
- Key light at 30-45 degrees to the subject, slightly above eye level
- The nose shadow should fall at about 45 degrees toward the corner of the mouth
- Fill light or reflector on the opposite side

Best for: Most subjects, especially women and children
Flatters: Most face shapes
Avoid for: Subjects with very long noses (the shadow can exaggerate length)

Pro tip: Loop lighting is the safest and most flattering pattern for most subjects. Start here and experiment with other patterns as you gain confidence.

3. Butterfly Lighting

The look: A butterfly-shaped shadow under the nose, with the light coming from directly above and in front of the subject. Also called Paramount lighting.

Setup:
- Key light directly in front of the subject, above eye level
- The light should be high enough to create a shadow under the nose and chin
- A reflector below the subject's face fills in shadows

Best for: Fashion, beauty, glamour portraits
Flatters: Oval faces, high cheekbones
Avoid for: Round or very square faces (can make them look wider)

Pro tip: Butterfly lighting emphasizes cheekbones and creates a glamorous look. It was the signature lighting style of Hollywood's golden age.

4. Split Lighting

The look: The face is split exactly in half — one side lit, one side in shadow. The most dramatic pattern.

Setup:
- Key light at 90 degrees to the subject
- The light should be at head height or slightly above
- No fill light on the shadow side (or very minimal)

Best for: Dramatic, moody, artistic portraits
Flatters: Strong jawlines, angular faces
Avoid for: Most commercial portraits, subjects with skin imperfections (the shadow side hides them, but the lit side reveals everything)

Pro tip: Split lighting is powerful but can be unflattering. Use it intentionally for character studies, not for standard headshots.

5. Short Lighting

The look: The side of the face turned toward the camera is in shadow; the side turned away is lit. Creates a slimming effect.

Setup:
- Subject is turned 3/4 away from the camera
- Key light illuminates the side of the face further from the camera
- The camera side of the face falls into shadow

Best for: Slimming portraits, dramatic character studies
Flatters: Round and full faces (creates a slimming effect)
Avoid for: Very thin faces (can look gaunt)

Pro tip: Short lighting is the most slimming portrait pattern. It's excellent for subjects who are self-conscious about their weight or face shape.

Practice Exercise

Set up a single light and a willing subject. Work through all five patterns in order. For each pattern, take a photo and note:
- Where is the light in relation to the subject?
- Where does the shadow fall?
- What mood does the pattern create?
- Does it flatter or challenge the subject's face shape?

This exercise will teach you more about portrait lighting than reading 100 articles.
